Certifying a Digital Health Product
by Designing for Behaviour, Not Features

Nazata Bio — Digital hypertension management platform

The Organisation
A clinically sound digital health platform that had solved the medical problem and not the human one.

Nazata Bio is an Irish digital health company building a scalable digital system for personalised high blood pressure lifestyle therapy. The clinical logic was sound, the technical build was competent and the regulatory pathway was mapped.

What the product lacked was a defensible account of why a patient would open it on day 40. In a regulated medical device context, sustained engagement is the mechanism through which the product produces its clinical effect — which makes behavioural architecture a functional requirement of the device, not a growth concern.

The Problem
Hypertension is asymptomatic, so every conventional engagement mechanism works against the grain of motivation.

Global medication adherence in hypertension sits below 50%, and a decade of digital tools has not moved that number. Adherence behaviours in asymptomatic conditions receive no felt feedback: the patient who takes their medication and the patient who does not feel identical at the end of the day.

Conventional digital health UX answers this with reminders, streaks and progress bars. These are compliance mechanisms, and compliance mechanisms produce a reliable curve — high initial engagement, sharp decline at four to six weeks, abandonment. They position the patient as a subject of surveillance rather than an agent of their own health, which is the precise psychological posture that undermines self-management.

The Solution
Rebuild the engagement architecture on Self-Determination Theory.

Rather than adding engagement features to an existing product, the work restructured the psychological logic underneath it and derived features from that logic.

Self-Determination Theory supplied the architecture. Intrinsic motivation — the only kind that survives the absence of external feedback — depends on autonomy, competence and relatedness. Every existing and proposed feature was mapped against these three needs and classified as supporting, neutral or actively undermining. Several features that tested well in conventional usability terms were found to undermine autonomy, and were redesigned rather than removed.

Gamification was reconstructed on the same basis: Health Points, milestone medals and blood pressure drop targets became competence signals that make invisible physiological progress legible, rather than extrinsic rewards competing with the behaviour itself.

The Impact
The behavioural work became part of the regulatory case, not an adjunct to it.

Following integration of the behavioural design recommendations, the product is working towards certified medical device status and is preparing to enter clinical trials in Germany. Behavioural design is usually treated as a retention concern; here it formed part of the evidence that the product does what it claims to do.

The reframe from compliance-based monitoring to autonomy-based engagement changes what the product is: a system that builds patient capability rather than one that observes patient failure. In a condition where the clinical effect depends entirely on behaviour sustained over years, that distinction determines whether the device works.

The methodology was presented at the Psychological Society of Ireland, Division of Behaviour Analysis (2025), placing the work into the professional evidence base.

Long-term projection

Longer term. Hypertension affects approximately 1.28 billion adults worldwide and is the leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular mortality. A digital therapeutic that sustains engagement past the six-week abandonment cliff addresses a failure mode common to the entire digital chronic disease category. The German clinical trial pathway opens the EU regulated digital therapeutics market, where reimbursement frameworks such as DiGA increasingly require demonstrated real-world engagement alongside clinical efficacy — a requirement that behavioural architecture, not feature volume, is what actually satisfies.

Process
Five stages, end to end.
  1. 01
    Behavioural product audit
    Systematic evaluation of the existing application against adherence and motivation frameworks, classifying every interaction as supporting, neutral or undermining relative to intrinsic motivation.
  2. 02
    Cognitive walkthrough
    Structured walkthrough across all primary user journeys, mapping where cognitive load exceeded the capability of the target clinical population.
  3. 03
    Behavioural architecture redesign
    User journeys, notification logic, progress feedback and social features restructured against the three Self-Determination Theory needs, with gamification rebuilt as competence signalling.
  4. 04
    Friction reduction design
    Adherence behaviours restructured to remove friction at the daily logging step and align prompts to natural decision windows.
  5. 05
    Engineering handover
    Findings delivered as prioritised, implementable design recommendations tied to specific interface elements, then integrated into the product build.

The Organisation
A national infrastructure programme with 400+ locations and no shared evidence base for who its users were.

Connected Hubs is the Government of Ireland’s national remote working infrastructure programme, delivered with the Western Development Commission. It spans 400+ registered locations across cities, market towns, coastal communities and Gaeltacht regions, serving corporate remote employees, sole traders, freelancers, community organisations and rural entrepreneurs.

The capital investment had been made. What had not been built was the evidence infrastructure: any systematic account of who was walking through those doors, what kept them and what quietly drove them away.

The Problem
Four hundred managers were making the same commercial decisions from intuition, in isolation, with no way to learn from each other.

Hub managers were making genuinely difficult commercial decisions — pricing, event programming, marketing spend, onboarding design — entirely from instinct, because no diagnostic framework existed for them to use.

The consequence compounded in two directions. Individually, managers repeatedly solved the same problem without understanding why it recurred, so the solution never held. At network level, a strategy that worked in an urban enterprise hub failed in a rural community hub and no shared vocabulary existed to explain the difference. Four hundred managers were independently rediscovering the same lessons with no mechanism to capture any of it.

Underneath sat a measurement problem: the available data described users demographically, and demographics do not predict behaviour. Two users with identical profiles could hold opposed motivations, and designing for their average served neither.

The Solution
A multi-method research study producing five psychologically grounded personas and an operational toolkit built on them.

The study was designed to produce something a hub manager could use on a Tuesday, and that constraint shaped the method throughout.

Qualitative depth and quantitative breadth were combined deliberately: interviews and ethnographic observation surfaced motivational texture that surveys systematically miss, while a network-wide survey established which patterns held at scale.

The output was five behavioural personas — the Time-Tied Trooper, Autonomous Agent, Entrepreneurial Explorer, Creative Cadet and Emerging Enigma. Each captures motivational architecture, decision triggers, friction points, communication preferences and the specific moments at which hub habits are most likely to change in either direction.

These became the backbone of a Customer Experience toolkit deployed nationally: journey maps, engagement checklists, communication templates and segmentation guides written so a manager with no behavioural science training could apply them immediately.

The Impact
The research replaced intuition with a shared national vocabulary across 400+ operators.

The toolkit was adopted across 400+ workspaces nationwide. Decisions about events, pricing, marketing and onboarding could now be made by asking a specific, answerable question: which persona are we designing for, and where in their journey are we intervening?

The more durable outcome was linguistic. A network of 400 isolated operators acquired a shared vocabulary for describing their users — which is what makes knowledge transferable between hubs. A manager in Donegal and a manager in Dublin can now describe the same user type in the same terms and compare what worked. The research converted 400 separate learning curves into one.

Hub managers continued referencing the personas as a working planning tool months after the research concluded, and the study became the foundational evidence base for the Engagement Lab.

A key highlight of the summit was Padraig Walsh from ChangeAble Behaviour Solutions, who presented his standout research on hub user profiles, offering valuable insights into how different user groups engage with hubs and what drives their decision-making.

— Western Development Commission Report, 2024
Long-term projection

Longer term. The persona framework is extensible: as remote work patterns evolve, new segments can be added against the same behavioural architecture rather than requiring a fresh study. The methodology transfers directly to other distributed public infrastructure — libraries, enterprise centres, community facilities and regional innovation hubs — where the same structural problem recurs: significant capital investment in physical infrastructure, minimal investment in understanding the behaviour that determines whether it is used.

Process
Five stages, end to end.
  1. 01
    Mixed-methods research design
    Study designed to combine qualitative depth and quantitative breadth, with instruments built per data source and an explicit requirement that outputs be operationally usable by non-specialists.
  2. 02
    Field data collection
    Semi-structured interviews and ethnographic observation across urban, rural, enterprise and community hub contexts, alongside a network-wide quantitative survey.
  3. 03
    Analysis and integration
    Inductive thematic analysis of qualitative data integrated with descriptive statistics and cross-tabulation of survey responses to produce behaviourally coherent segments.
  4. 04
    Persona development and validation
    Five personas constructed from the integrated dataset and validated against the COM-B model to ensure each pointed toward actionable intervention rather than description.
  5. 05
    Toolkit design and national rollout
    Customer journey maps, engagement frameworks and the full CX toolkit designed, produced and distributed across the 400+ hub network.

The Organisation
Ireland’s national remote working network, and the gap between having infrastructure and knowing how to use it.

The Engagement Lab was commissioned as the operational answer to the persona research that preceded it. The research established who was using the national hub network and why. The Engagement Lab existed to answer the harder question: how do you get 400 independent operators to change what they do on Monday morning.

This is a change management problem before it is a training problem. Hub managers are not employees of a central organisation — they cannot be mandated or performance-managed into new behaviour. Any intervention had to work through voluntary adoption.

The Problem
Traditional professional development fails for exactly the population that needs it most: isolated operators with no time and no peers.

Conventional capability programmes assume protected time, an organisational mandate, colleagues to discuss material with and a manager tracking completion. A hub manager has none of these — frequently the entire staff of their organisation, working reactively, with no peer group.

The result across the sector is well documented: high sign-up, sharp attrition at week three, near-zero behavioural transfer. Content is consumed and never applied, because the programme ends where the difficult work of application begins.

Compounding this, frameworks delivered abstractly do not survive contact with a specific commercial context. The translation step — from framework to this hub, this segment, this Tuesday — is where almost all capability programmes silently fail, and it is the step almost none are designed to support.

The Solution
A longitudinal community of practice with application built into its structure, not left to the participant afterwards.

The Engagement Lab was designed as a Longitudinal Innovation Intensive Development model. Participants were treated not as a training audience but as a distributed research network generating evidence together.

Between sessions, participants ran live engagement experiments in their own hubs and brought real data back to the cohort for collective analysis. This inverted the usual failure mode: application was not left to the participant after the programme ended, it was the mechanism through which the programme operated.

Delivery ran across five integrated modalities — a self-paced platform, three facilitated live sessions, an in-person workshop day, peer-led Practice Pod capstones and individual coaching — each carrying a distinct function rather than repeating content in different formats.

An AI workflow integration component was built into the curriculum, piloted with approximately 22 hubs and small businesses whose owners had little prior exposure to digital systems. The population least likely to adopt AI tools independently is the population for whom the productivity gain is largest.

The Impact
The programme exceeded every participation target and produced measurable commercial change in participating businesses.

The programme achieved a 92% overall quality score, exceeded participation targets by 133% and reached an 88% adoption rate for the digital tools introduced. Community data showed 78.6% of posts receiving replies at an average of 6.3 replies per active thread — sustained peer dialogue rather than passive consumption.

Commercial outcomes reported by participants were substantial. One hub ran a second iteration of an event following the programme and recorded twice the vendors, twice the attendees and double the revenue, alongside a significant increase in five-star reviews generated through newly implemented feedback touchpoints.

Running a second iteration of an event post-Engagement Lab resulted in twice as many vendors, twice as many attendees, and double the revenue. Implementing additional consumer feedback touchpoints led to a significant increase in 5-star Google reviews and generated valuable testimonials now being repurposed in digital marketing strategies.

Long-term projection

Longer term. The programme created a standing peer network that continues to operate independently of any facilitated session — the difference between a training intervention and durable capability infrastructure. The LIID model is replicable across any distributed network of independent operators facing common problems in isolation: franchise networks, community enterprise, regional development programmes, primary care practices. A pre-session workshop was facilitated at the National Hub Summit 2025, positioning the model within national policy conversation.

Process
Five stages, end to end.
  1. 01
    Programme architecture design
    Five-modality blended structure designed so each format performed a distinct function — content, application, trust-building, peer generation and individual coaching kept deliberately separate.
  2. 02
    Curriculum and AI module build
    Behavioural frameworks translated into hub-specific application, with an AI workflow integration module piloted for owners with minimal prior digital systems exposure.
  3. 03
    Delivery and facilitation
    Six-month delivery across platform, live sessions, in-person workshop, peer capstone pods and one-to-one coaching, with operations managed end to end across all 16 participants.
  4. 04
    Engagement measurement
    Participant activity tracked across module completion, submission rates, attendance and community contribution, with discussions coded by comment type to test whether genuine peer knowledge exchange was occurring.
  5. 05
    Evaluation and impact reporting
    Multi-instrument evaluation combining quantitative scoring and qualitative synthesis, compiled into the final impact report submitted to the Western Development Commission.

The Organisation
Ireland’s largest financial institution, undertaking values-led cultural transformation at enterprise scale.

The client is one of Ireland’s largest financial institutions, employing approximately 10,000 people across retail, corporate, operations and technology functions. The organisation was in a period of strategic cultural realignment and had done the substantial work of articulating its values clearly.

The brief was precise and unusually honest. Leadership did not want another communications campaign. They wanted the values to become observable in everyday decision-making — visible in how a colleague handles an awkward trade-off when nobody senior is watching.

The Problem
Organisational values are almost universally communicated and almost universally ignored. The failure is structural.

Every large organisation has values, drafted carefully and communicated repeatedly. Recall is typically reasonable; behavioural influence is typically negligible.

Values do not operate in the situations where they are communicated. They operate in ambiguity — where two legitimate priorities conflict, where the fair action is slower than the expedient one. A poster cannot reach that moment because it describes values in the abstract, and the abstract is not where the decision happens.

Most values programmes are also facilitator-dependent, restricting them to senior cohorts and making 10,000-person reach economically impossible. There was a further credibility constraint: 10,000 employees have finely calibrated detectors for corporate content that does not describe their working reality. Generic externally-written scenarios would be identified within thirty seconds and fail on contact.

The Solution
Build the intervention entirely from staff data, then deliver it as a branching scenario game requiring no facilitator.

The programme was built in two phases, and the sequence mattered more than either phase alone.

Discovery first. Before any scenario was written, 45-minute facilitated focus groups were run with 90+ employees to surface what the values look like when enacted, and what they look like when quietly compromised. Staff described real tensions from their own working lives, producing raw material no external curriculum could supply.

Then narrative design. Findings were translated into Values in Action, a branching scenario game in which players navigate a character through workplace dilemmas drawn directly from that data. At each decision point the player chooses, encounters a consequence and is prompted to reflect — engaging intuitive judgement through narrative and deliberate reasoning through structured reflection.

Delivery was designed for self-directed asynchronous deployment, removing the facilitation bottleneck and making 10,000-person reach achievable in seven weeks rather than eighteen months.

The Impact
Full organisational reach in seven weeks, with content staff recognised as their own.

The programme reached all 10,000 employees within seven weeks of discovery commencing and scored an Exceeds Expectations rating in internal engagement measurement. For a values intervention at enterprise scale in a regulated financial institution, that timeline is the headline operational result — achievable only because the facilitation dependency had been designed out.

The substantive outcome was recognition. Staff encountered scenarios that felt real because they were real, drawn from 90+ colleagues’ described experience. That recognition separates an intervention people complete from one people discuss afterwards, and it was a direct product of sequencing discovery before design.

The deeper result is a shared organisational vocabulary for values tensions. The situations now have names, the tensions have been rehearsed collectively, and the conversation has a starting point.

Long-term projection

Longer term. The discovery-to-narrative methodology transfers to any organisation-wide behavioural challenge where the gap is application rather than awareness: conduct risk, psychological safety, inclusive behaviour, safety culture. Because the scenario architecture is modular, the same delivery infrastructure can carry new content without rebuilding. Following this programme, a further engagement was scoped with the same client on enterprise Microsoft Copilot adoption, covering adoption, engagement and safe and ethical use at scale.

Process
Five stages, end to end.
  1. 01
    Discovery focus groups
    45-minute facilitated group elicitation sessions with 90+ employees across the organisation, designed to extract lived behavioural data on values enactment and compromise within severe constraints on staff time.
  2. 02
    Data analysis and theme selection
    Systematic coding of discovery output to identify the recurring values tensions with the highest behavioural frequency and organisational consequence.
  3. 03
    Scenario architecture design
    Coded findings translated into a branching decision structure with multiple pathways and endings, each branch representing a genuinely defensible choice rather than an obvious right answer.
  4. 04
    Build and stakeholder alignment
    Interactive product built and aligned across HR, communications and senior leadership throughout scoping, development and sign-off.
  5. 05
    Organisation-wide rollout and reporting
    Self-directed asynchronous deployment across all 10,000 staff within seven weeks, followed by structured evaluation and impact reporting to senior stakeholders.

The Organisation
Ireland’s specialist behaviour-led CPD platform, built by practising clinicians rather than content producers.

Actualise Academy is an EPV-accredited online professional development platform serving Irish teachers, SNAs and early-years practitioners. Its sixteen courses span behaviour support, anxiety management, autism-inclusive practice, teacher wellbeing and neuroscience-informed teaching.

The founding premise was that Irish educators needed CPD designed by practising behaviour specialists rather than generic content providers, and the content quality reflected that. What had not kept pace was the commercial and analytical infrastructure required to convert quality into sustained growth.

The Problem
Excellent content, thousands of members, and no visibility into why anyone was buying.

The academy had genuine assets: EPV accreditation, credible expert authorship, national media presence and deep course content. What it lacked was the evidence infrastructure to make decisions with.

With primitive data tracking and understanding, decisions defaulted to intuition. Marketing spend was allocated without attribution. Course development priorities were set without usage evidence. Acquisition relied on Google search, EPV listings and word of mouth — fragile, unscalable and entirely outside the platform’s control.

A second-order constraint compounded this. The academy is a small operation, so any growth system had to be maintainable by a very small team — ruling out solutions dependent on continuous original content production. The constraint was not ambition. It was capacity.

The Solution
Build one place where every metric can be compared, then let AI do the reconciling.

The work began with instrumentation rather than campaigns. The platform’s numbers existed, but they lived in separate places — course sales in one system, enrolment in another, campaign performance in a third, email metrics in a fourth. Nobody could see them together, so nobody could compare them.

The answer was a single place to bring all of it together: an Excel-based year-on-year model layered onto monthly KPI dashboards, designed as a checking layer rather than a report. Its function is comparison — this month against last, this course against that one, this campaign against the one before it, this year against the same period last year. Enrolment trends by course and cohort, campaign-level attribution, email sequence performance and seasonal purchasing patterns all became visible side by side for the first time.

AI-assisted analysis sits on top of it. Rather than manually reconciling and interpreting each month’s figures, AI tools are used to synthesise across the data sources, surface the comparisons that moved and flag what changed and why — turning a reporting task that would otherwise consume days into something a small team can run monthly.

That visibility immediately changed decisions. Drop-off points in the learner journey were identified from usage data and course design and product development were reprioritised accordingly. Marketing spend was directed and tracked against budget using conversion data rather than assumption.

The same AI-assisted approach addressed the capacity ceiling on content: existing television, radio and podcast appearances were repurposed into structured social content, sustaining a consistent publishing rhythm without the volume of original production a small team cannot maintain.

The Impact
Membership more than tripled, revenue grew 49% year on year, and the platform recorded its strongest sales period to date.

Active membership grew from 3,000 to over 10,300 and continues to grow. Revenue grew 49% year on year, and the platform delivered the highest sales period in its history.

The mechanism behind those numbers was not a marketing campaign. It was the conversion of scattered usage data into comparable, checkable product decisions. Identifying where learners dropped out and reprioritising course design improved the product, which improved retention, which improved word of mouth, which reduced dependence on paid acquisition. The dashboard was not a reporting artefact — it was the checking layer every subsequent decision was run against.

The AI-assisted content system solved the constraint that would otherwise have capped growth, allowing a very small team to sustain a publishing rhythm that would conventionally require several full-time staff. The growth is maintainable rather than dependent on unsustainable effort.

Long-term projection

Longer term. The measurement infrastructure and AI-assisted content system are permanent assets that continue producing decisions rather than one-time gains. With attribution established, marketing spend can be scaled against known conversion rates instead of estimated ones. The model — instrument first, let data direct product, use AI to remove the capacity ceiling — transfers directly to any small specialist education business facing the same constraint: strong expertise, weak commercial infrastructure, no headcount to bridge the gap.

The Organisation
A technology product built to encode scarce expert judgement into an always-available system.

Projet Merge is an early-stage adaptive decision-support system in active development, comprising a mobile client for frontline users and a web dashboard for organisational leads.

The technical problem is expertise distribution. Specialist domain knowledge exists but is delivered synchronously by a scarce human expert on a monthly cycle, while demand for it is continuous and unpredictable. The system encodes that judgement into an adaptive routing architecture that resolves a user’s situation to a specific, evidence-grounded output in under two minutes, with structured escalation where the situation exceeds what encoded logic can safely handle.

The Problem
Three technical gaps compound, and no product on the market closes them within a single system.

Availability. Expert support is delivered synchronously on a monthly cycle. Between sessions the system has no coverage, and a non-specialist user makes a consequential decision with no support layer available.

Retrieval. Training platforms store knowledge but provide no retrieval mechanism at the point of need. A user under time pressure cannot search a course library. The requirement is a structured query path resolving to a single actionable output in seconds, not a document to be read.

Data. Organisational leads have no structured dataset on what their teams encounter. The signal is trapped in individual memory and unstructured free-text logs, which are not queryable. Without structured capture at the point of interaction, pattern detection is impossible and governance stays reactive by construction.

Existing products address one gap each. Closing all three requires a system where the capture mechanism and the support mechanism are the same interaction.

The Solution
A two-surface system architecture where structured data capture is a by-product of delivering user value.

The client-side interface is a structured triage flow rather than a content library. A point-of-incident flag triggers a five-question broad filter routing to one of approximately ten themes, followed by narrow filtering that resolves to a solution card — plain-language, immediately actionable output designed to be read and applied by someone under pressure in under two minutes.

Every interaction feeds a feedback loop — tried, resolved, not resolved — and an escalation pathway to human consultation where required. Interaction flows are built around the error-prone decision points identified through five years of delivering the service manually.

The dashboard aggregates anonymised theme frequency across the organisation, enabling proactive pattern identification before escalation and generating templated reporting structured to meet regulatory documentation requirements. Governance intelligence is generated as a by-product of frontline use rather than requiring separate administrative effort — the design decision that makes it survive contact with an under-resourced team.

The Impact
A system designed to convert scarce expertise into continuously available infrastructure.

Projet Merge is in active development, currently at UX and go-to-market strategic stage. The significance sits in what has been architected rather than what has been deployed.

The product demonstrates a transferable proposition: five years of specialist expertise, previously available to five organisations one afternoon a month, can be encoded into an architecture available continuously. This is not automation of expert judgement — it is structured triage that routes a non-specialist to an evidence-based starting point and escalates cleanly when the situation exceeds what logic can safely handle.

The governance dimension converts documentation from an administrative burden into an operational asset: an organisational lead gains the ability to intervene on a pattern before it becomes a finding.

A critical assumption — whether frontline users can reliably self-classify a situation at the point of logging — has been formally documented as requiring validation before launch. Naming that risk explicitly is itself an outcome: the difference between a product built on a hope and one built on a testable proposition.

Long-term projection

Longer term. The architecture is designed for extension into adjacent regulated settings facing the same three gaps. Enterprise Ireland and Local Enterprise Office funding pathways are being pursued. If validated, the model addresses a structural constraint across health and social care globally: specialist expertise is scarce, expensive and unevenly distributed, while demand for it is continuous and distributed everywhere.

The Organisation
A multidisciplinary urban health initiative asking what actually shapes health behaviour in a city.

Cities for Better Health is a multidisciplinary urban health initiative at the Centre for Global Health, Trinity College Dublin, focused on chronic disease prevention at community level.

Chronic disease prevention is conventionally approached through clinical risk factors and individual advice. The premise here is different: that health behaviour in a city is shaped by environment, social structure, institutional design and community norms, and that intervention design must therefore begin with how people actually live.

The Problem
Researching health behaviour across children, teachers, institutions and adults simultaneously, without flattening any of them.

The initiative spans children, teachers, school stakeholders and adult community participants. Each group holds a different relationship to health behaviour, a different vocabulary for describing it and a fundamentally different capacity to engage with research instruments.

Standard practice is to design one instrument and adapt it downward, and it systematically fails. A journal that works for an adult is unusable by a child. A consent process appropriate for a teacher is ethically inadequate for a minor. A focus group protocol designed for peer discussion does not function across an institutional hierarchy.

The second constraint is ethical rather than methodological: research spanning child and adult participants requires materially different consent architectures, safeguarding considerations and data governance running in parallel within a single study.

The third is that findings must actually inform intervention design. Research producing an accurate description and no actionable direction has not met the brief.

The Solution
Design research materials per audience rather than adapting one instrument, and run consent as a parallel architecture.

Participant-facing materials are designed separately for each audience rather than adapted from a single master instrument — including age-appropriate journals for children alongside distinct materials for teachers, school stakeholders and adult community participants, with language, complexity and format tailored per group across the 160+ participant pipeline.

Data collection combines semi-structured interviews and focus groups with diverse urban stakeholders, with protocols adjusted for group composition and institutional dynamics. Analysis applies thematic analysis in NVivo to surface insights capable of informing intervention design rather than only describing current behaviour.

Ethical compliance, informed consent and GDPR data governance are managed as a parallel architecture across the multidisciplinary team, with consent processes adapted separately for child and adult groups. Project workflows are coordinated with the Principal Investigator using agile iterative cycles, allowing methodology to adapt in response to emerging findings.

The Impact
Research designed from the outset to produce intervention direction, not only description.

The work is ongoing, and its significance to date lies in the research infrastructure established.

Designing materials per audience rather than adapting downward has produced a usable dataset across all four participant groups, including children — a group frequently excluded from urban health research precisely because instrument design for them is harder. Their inclusion matters: childhood is where the behavioural patterns underlying adult chronic disease are established, and research that excludes children misses the intervention point with the greatest long-term leverage.

The parallel consent and safeguarding architecture demonstrates that multi-generational community research can be conducted rigorously and ethically within a single study — the practical obstacle that most often causes such studies to narrow to adults only.

Thematic analysis is surfacing insights structured explicitly to inform chronic disease prevention intervention design, with the intervention question in view from the outset rather than retrofitted.

Long-term projection

Longer term. Chronic disease represents the dominant share of health system burden across high-income countries, and prevention at community level is the only intervention point with population-scale leverage. Research capturing how health behaviour is shaped across generations within the same urban environment supports intervention design addressing environment and social structure rather than individual advice. The multi-audience materials methodology is directly reusable by any community health research programme facing the same participant-diversity constraint.

The Organisation
A hospital memory clinic and a national question about who actually gets access to cognitive rehabilitation.

This work was delivered at Tallaght University Hospital, within the ProBrain Research Lab and in partnership with the Alzheimer Society of Ireland, as part of a nationally funded study on the accessibility of cognitive rehabilitation in Ireland.

Goal-oriented Cognitive Rehabilitation is one of few non-pharmacological interventions with a credible evidence base for maintaining everyday functioning in early-stage dementia. The evidence exists. Access does not. The national question was structural: what prevents a person diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer’s from receiving an intervention known to help.

The Problem
Designing an intervention for a population whose defining characteristic is impairment of the exact functions the intervention depends on.

Cognitive rehabilitation asks a participant to set a meaningful functional goal, hold it across weeks, apply strategies between sessions and self-monitor progress. Every one of those requirements draws on memory, executive function and self-monitoring — precisely the capacities affected by the condition being treated.

Generic programme design cannot solve this. Materials pitched at standard reading levels exclude participants. Sessions paced for a research schedule outpace comprehension. Consent processes designed for cognitively intact adults become ethically fragile when capacity fluctuates day to day.

A second constraint is that the patient is not the only user. Family caregivers carry much of the between-session implementation, and their capacity and understanding materially determine whether the intervention works. Designing for the patient alone reliably produces an intervention that fails at home.

The Solution
An intervention designed around the actual cognitive and social conditions of the people receiving it, evaluated for individual-level effect.

A six-week Goal-oriented Cognitive Rehabilitation intervention was designed, delivered and evaluated for individuals with early-stage Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias.

Delivery was adapted continuously to the participant rather than the protocol. Method, language and pacing were adjusted to cognitive presentation while maintaining protocol integrity — the balance that determines whether a study produces usable data or excludes the participants it exists to serve. Consent was treated as an ongoing process rather than a signature, reflecting fluctuating capacity.

Family caregivers were engaged as participants in the intervention rather than observers of it, supporting adherence in the home environment where the functional goals actually had to be achieved.

Evaluation used single-subject research design alongside pre/post methodology. In a heterogeneous dementia population, group averages conceal exactly the individual-level treatment effects that matter clinically. Single-subject design surfaces whether this person improved on their goal.

The Impact
A contributing study cited in the World Alzheimer Report 2025.

The study contributed to the international evidence base on cognitive rehabilitation accessibility and was cited in the World Alzheimer Report 2025, published by Alzheimer’s Disease International. For a nationally funded accessibility study, citation in the sector’s principal global report is the clearest signal that the work informed the field rather than only the funder.

The methodological contribution matters as much as the finding. Demonstrating that a structured protocol can be delivered with fidelity to a cognitively impaired population addresses the practical objection most often raised against scaling these interventions.

The caregiver-inclusive design produced a further insight with direct service implications: interventions treating the caregiver as an implementation partner rather than an accompanying relative are substantially more likely to survive the transition from clinic to home.

Long-term projection

Longer term. Dementia prevalence in Ireland is projected to rise substantially while specialist neuropsychology capacity remains constrained. Evidence that structured cognitive rehabilitation can be delivered with fidelity, and adapted for accessibility, supports expanding delivery beyond specialist memory clinics into community and primary care. The design principles established — adaptive pacing, caregiver inclusion, ongoing consent, individual-level evaluation — transfer directly to any intervention designed for cognitively vulnerable populations, including the digital tools now entering this space.
